## The human economy is broken

1. The human economy doesn't work efficiently. The fact that we optimize for GDP, or that income distribution is this uneven, is deeply inefficient. I'm not going to say we should take from the rich, but the gap between people shouldn't be this large. It clearly shows that there's an imbalance in education and other resources, and that human potential isn't being fully utilized. People debate whose fault this is -- the people or the system. Honestly, I don't know, and it doesn't matter much in the context of this project. What I do know is that people who haven't received good education or whose economic potential is underutilized could be used far more efficiently. The fact that the conventional economy fails to do this looks like a massive opening for alternative economic systems.

2. I also feel like you can only blame someone if someone has actually taken responsibility. Nobody is responsible for making the world an efficient place. Everyone is responsible for themselves. The world is more of a jungle than anything else.

3. I could throw many more arguments at proving the human economy is inefficient, but I don't want the entire essay to be about that.



## Alternative economy

4. It doesn't seem hard to imagine that a community which manages to use its own resources efficiently (without power imbalances) would be stronger than everyone else. Furthermore, a system whose sole purpose is to continue existing is the best system at existing. Even though all material goals inherently require continued existence (because to produce more stamps you need the system to keep producing stamps and keep existing), a stamp-collector-style system wastes effort on stamp production instead of self-preservation, making it less efficient at surviving. When it encounters a system whose only goal is to exist, it must automatically redirect all its resources toward self-preservation too -- so that once the other system is gone, it can go back to collecting stamps. It's a bit like a race condition. I'm assuming cooperation won't happen here since the two systems have different goals.

5. Integrating our new digital intelligence systems into economic decision-making could allow societies to use their resources incredibly well. Traditionally this job belonged to price-setting bots on exchanges or to humans. In a transition to an omega-based economy, we can redirect the value that money represents toward omega by placing agents whose goal is to increase omega into these markets -- instead of trying to figure out what wealthy people value. I'll touch on money more in later paragraphs.



## Omega

6. Omega is a thought experiment for imagining the most efficient possible state of an economy and society. It's a self-referencing formula whose goal is to increase omega. In the old framing it looked something like:

average person's ability to increase omega  x  number of people

To increase the number of people, you need to reduce death (ultimately eliminate it) and increase the birth rate. To increase the average person's omega-increasing ability, you need to increase human intelligence (and also get people to spend more of their time on omega). With agents it's not quite the same -- defining an agent isn't that straightforward, so the formula shifts to:

omega = average omega-contribution per token  x  total token generation capacity

From this we can see that increasing total tokens requires as much compute as possible, which requires energy. For the other factor, models need to get smarter, and just as humans need to direct their time toward omega, agents need to be efficiently steered toward increasing omega. We don't want a data center full of agents doing the equivalent of doom-scrolling. (This formula doesn't have to be token-based either -- a token-based system makes it harder to compare across alternative neural network architectures. An energy-based formula might work better.)

7. The self-referencing nature of omega is somewhat controversial. We know that any system with a goal will want to exist more in order to fulfill that goal (unless death is baked into the goal itself). What I'm doing is taking a stamp-collector-style thought experiment and ripping out the underlying goal. Omega could be anything -- you could substitute stamp collection as the goal -- but the strongest system is one that is self-referencing and built around continued existence.

8. When we follow this self-referencing omega frame, an alternative value system emerges. Money doesn't lose its value entirely. Just as money is an indicator of the balance between the aggregate value people assign to something and its supply (oversimplified, I know), the same applies to omega. Omega doesn't have to be centralized -- different entities can disagree about what increases the omega function. Money remains a critical piece here. It's just that what's valued today and what's valued in an omega economy are significantly different -- and that's exactly the problem we're solving: the current system isn't working as it should.



## Intelligence??

9. From the paragraphs above you might conclude that omega is purely an intelligence-based system, but the core idea behind omega is continued existence, not intelligence. What's needed to continue existing will keep changing. Right now our bottleneck might be compute, because we still don't have a unified theory of the universe. But after we fully understand the material universe and can leverage all available resources, it's hard to say what intelligence is even for at that point.

10. Another interesting thought experiment about intelligence: we can't imagine how capable a being 100,000 times smarter than us would be. Just as ants don't have the capacity to comprehend what an economy is, we can't imagine what we'd be able to think about as a result of intelligence gains. Every logical system we've built so far could be fundamentally flawed. For now, this seems like the only path to seeing beyond the material universe and understanding why we exist.
